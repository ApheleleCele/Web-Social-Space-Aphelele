4IR, 5IR, and where technology and society are actually heading

From the Fourth to the Fifth Industrial Revolution

A brief exploration of how the Fourth Industrial Revolution's technology-first momentum is giving way to a more human-centred Fifth Industrial Revolution — and what the World Economic Forum's own risk data suggests about how urgent that shift has become.

The Fourth Industrial Revolution

A fusion of the physical, digital and biological

Klaus Schwab, founder of the World Economic Forum, describes the Fourth Industrial Revolution (4IR) as the emergence of "cyber-physical systems" — a fusion of technologies blurring the line between the physical, digital and biological worlds, building on the computing and internet infrastructure of the Third Industrial Revolution but embedding technology far more deeply into society and even the human body itself, through genome editing, new forms of machine intelligence, and governance tools such as blockchain (World Economic Forum, 2016).

What distinguishes the 4IR from earlier waves of technological change is less the individual technologies than their combined speed, scope and systemic reach: change is arriving faster, touching more sectors simultaneously, and reshaping entire systems rather than single processes. This creates real benefits — Herweijer et al. (2018) describe how G20 countries can use 4IR technologies to support sustainable development, while Bianchini et al. (2018) show how digital transformation is enabling more circular, less wasteful production models. But the same speed and scope that produce these gains also drive the 4IR's central risk: disruption to employment, biological and ethical questions raised by genome editing, and a real danger that these gains concentrate among those who can already access and afford the technology, widening existing inequality rather than closing it.

The Fifth Industrial Revolution

Putting the human back at the centre

Gauri and Van Eerden (2019) argue that the 4IR's weakness is precisely its technology-first focus: by centring what machines can do, it risks dehumanising the outcomes it produces. The Fifth Industrial Revolution (5IR) responds with a philosophy of harmonious human–machine collaboration, aimed at the well-being of society, companies, employees and customers together, rather than efficiency alone. Ziatdinov et al. (2024) connect this to the broader vision of Society 5.0, where 5IR-aligned technology is meant to co-evolve alongside healthcare, the economy, education, cybersecurity, smart cities and the workforce — improvement across society, not just industry. Anil (2025) frames this as a matter of technology convergence: 5IR isn't a single new invention, but existing 4IR technologies increasingly designed around people rather than around themselves.

Mapping the balance: the Noble et al. matrix

Noble et al. (2022) offer a practical way to evaluate whether a given use of technology is actually achieving this balance, plotting "human strengths" against "technological strengths" to produce four quadrants. A technology-focused quadrant (e.g. automated customer care centres) relies on automation over empathy or judgement even where human traits are still needed. A human-technology underutilisation quadrant covers mundane, repetitive work — packing, lifting, stock monitoring, fast-food frontline roles — done without technological support that could ease it. A human-focused quadrant (elderly care, childcare, small family-run businesses) requires empathy and problem-solving that technology rarely assists, even where it plausibly could help. The goal quadrant is human–machine collaboration: humans handling emotion- and creativity-based work while machines handle the rest — what Gauri and Van Eerden (2019) describe as "humans and machines dancing together" to improve societal well-being.

A more critical view. Not every account of the 5IR is this optimistic. Costa et al. (2022) directly question whether the Fifth Industrial Revolution genuinely benefits global civil society, or whether "harmonious collaboration" mainly describes wealthier economies and firms already able to invest in both advanced technology and the human-centred design layered on top of it — leaving the underutilisation and human-focused quadrants, disproportionately staffed by lower-paid workers in poorer regions, without the resources to move toward genuine collaboration at all. Treated uncritically, the 5IR risks becoming an aspiration mainly available to those who already benefited most from the 4IR.
What the World Economic Forum's own risk data shows

The World Economic Forum's Global Risks Report is a useful check on how urgent this shift actually is. Comparing the 2025 edition (survey conducted September–October 2024) against the 2026 edition (published January 2026) shows a clear movement on the two-year outlook: geoeconomic confrontation jumped from 9th to 1st place — the single largest riser — while societal polarization and the erosion of human rights and freedoms also moved up the ranking. State-based armed conflict, extreme weather, and involuntary migration all fell in relative severity over the same period (World Economic Forum, 2025, 2026).

On the ten-year outlook, environmental risks still dominate the top three in both editions, but misinformation and disinformation climbed from 5th to 4th, and adverse outcomes of AI technologies held a firm position just behind it — one of the fastest-rising risks identified in the 2026 report overall. Read together with the 4IR/5IR discussion above, this is a fairly direct signal: the technologies at the centre of the 4IR (AI chief among them) are increasingly seen by global experts as a long-term societal risk in their own right, which is exactly the gap the 5IR's human-centred philosophy is trying to close.

As an IS&T professional, this reframes the practical question: not simply "can this system be automated," but "which quadrant does this system push society toward." A system that lands in the technology-focused or underutilisation quadrant is arguably a design failure even if it works technically — the aim, per the 5IR, is deliberately building toward collaboration, while staying honest about who currently has the resources to get there.
