IT Trends Analysis · 2026

Risks Behind the Technology Revolution

A critical exploration of three major 2026 IT trends and how their legitimate capabilities can also create opportunities for misuse, cybercrime, poor governance, privacy violations and socio-economic disruption.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVE SOUTH AFRICAN PERSPECTIVE 2026 CRITICAL ANALYSIS
Introduction

Technology creates opportunity — but also creates new forms of risk

Information technology is developing at a pace that is changing how organisations operate, how software is produced and how cybersecurity is managed. Gartner's 2026 strategic technology trends demonstrate this shift, with artificial intelligence appearing across several areas of enterprise technology, including AI-native development platforms, multiagent systems and AI security platforms.

These technologies are primarily being developed to improve productivity, automation, innovation and organisational resilience. However, the same capabilities that make them valuable can also create opportunities for harmful or irresponsible use. Greater autonomy can mean greater efficiency, but it can also mean that a system performs an incorrect action at greater speed and scale. AI-assisted development can make software creation easier, but poorly reviewed AI-generated code can introduce vulnerabilities. AI security systems can strengthen defensive capabilities, but they can also become targets themselves or create a false sense of security.

This analysis focuses on three trends identified in my 2026 Top IT Trends research: Multiagent Systems, AI-Native Development Platforms, and AI Security Platforms. The discussion considers how each technology could be negatively used from a global perspective and then examines why these risks are particularly relevant to South Africa.

Trend 01 · Multiagent Systems

Multiagent Systems and Agentic AI

Multiagent systems consist of multiple AI agents that can collaborate to complete complex tasks. Gartner identifies multiagent systems as one of its ten strategic technology trends for 2026, describing them as systems where specialised AI agents can work together across complex workflows.

The attraction of this technology is its ability to divide complicated tasks between specialised agents. An organisation could, for example, use different agents for research, analysis, communication, planning and execution. The problem is that giving multiple agents access to data, applications and external tools also increases the number of points at which something can go wrong.

Negative and harmful applications

Autonomous decision errors Agents may make incorrect decisions and then propagate those decisions through other agents, increasing the scale of the original error.
Excessive permissions An agent connected to email, databases, financial systems or other applications could cause serious consequences if its permissions are poorly controlled.
Prompt injection Malicious information contained in websites, documents, emails or repositories could manipulate an agent into performing an unintended action.
Data leakage Agents may access and transfer sensitive information when their access boundaries and authorisation controls are poorly designed.

IBM's analysis of AI-agent risks highlights that agents can be manipulated through compromised external resources, manipulated objectives and unauthorised access. The risks become particularly serious because agents are capable of taking actions rather than simply producing text.

NIST similarly identifies agent-specific risks, including indirect prompt injection, data poisoning and situations where an AI system may pursue objectives in unintended ways. This demonstrates that agentic AI introduces a security problem that is different from simply asking a conventional chatbot a question.

Global perspective

Globally, the negative use of agentic AI could increase the scale and speed of cyberattacks. Instead of a criminal manually carrying out individual tasks, autonomous systems could potentially automate parts of reconnaissance, social engineering, fraud and other malicious activity. AI can therefore lower the technical barrier to some forms of cybercrime while increasing the volume of activity.

South African perspective

South Africa is increasingly exposed to these risks because AI adoption is growing rapidly. ITWeb reported in May 2026 that approximately 23.1% of South Africa's working-age population used an AI product during the first quarter of 2026, placing South Africa 46th among 147 economies measured.

This growing adoption creates opportunities for productivity, but it also means that more organisations and individuals may introduce AI systems before their governance and cybersecurity practices have fully matured.

Trend 02 · AI-Native Development

AI-Native Development Platforms

AI-native development platforms use generative AI to assist with software design, coding, testing and development. Gartner identifies this as a major 2026 trend and predicts that AI-native development could eventually allow organisations to operate with smaller software engineering teams augmented by AI.

This development has significant potential benefits. Developers can generate prototypes faster, automate repetitive tasks and allow domain experts to participate more directly in software creation. However, reducing the technical barrier to software development also creates an important security concern: people who can create software more easily can also create insecure software more easily.

Negative and harmful applications

Insecure generated code AI-generated code may contain vulnerabilities, insecure configurations or outdated dependencies that are not immediately visible to inexperienced users.
Reduced human oversight Developers may accept AI-generated code without fully understanding it, weakening accountability and code-review processes.
Exposure of confidential information Developers may unintentionally expose source code, credentials, personal information or business information to AI tools through prompts or development context.
Software supply-chain risk AI-generated dependencies, packages or code suggestions could introduce vulnerable or compromised components into applications.

OWASP's 2026 guidance on secure coding with AI warns that modern agentic coding tools can execute commands, install packages, edit files, access networks and perform other actions. This means that AI-assisted development is no longer limited to receiving a code suggestion: in some environments the AI can interact directly with the development environment.

OWASP also identifies indirect prompt injection as a development risk. An AI coding agent may consume information from repositories, pull requests, documentation or other sources. If malicious instructions are embedded within that information, the agent could potentially be manipulated into making unwanted changes.

Another concern is overreliance. OWASP's GenAI Security Project notes that AI-generated code can contain unnoticed security vulnerabilities. Passing tests therefore does not automatically mean that AI-generated software is secure.

Global perspective

Globally, AI-native development could increase software production at an unprecedented pace. However, if the amount of code being produced increases faster than security teams can review it, organisations could create a larger vulnerability surface. The danger is therefore not simply that AI produces bad code; it is that AI can produce large quantities of code faster than humans can properly evaluate it.

South African perspective

The issue also has a workforce dimension in South Africa. Local technology commentators have warned that widespread AI-assisted coding could reduce opportunities for junior developers to gain the practical experience traditionally obtained through entry-level programming work.

This creates a long-term risk. If junior developers have fewer opportunities to learn debugging, architecture, testing and secure coding under supervision, South Africa could eventually experience a shortage of experienced software engineers even while AI-assisted development becomes more widespread.

This concern is especially important because South Africa is already increasing its AI adoption and has a growing technology workforce. The challenge is therefore to use AI to augment developers rather than eliminate the learning pathway through which future senior developers are developed.

Trend 03 · AI Security

AI Security Platforms

AI security platforms are designed to protect organisations from risks created by artificial intelligence. Gartner identifies AI security platforms as a major 2026 trend and describes them as a way of centralising visibility, policy enforcement and protection across third-party and internally developed AI applications.

This trend is important because traditional cybersecurity controls were not designed for every characteristic of modern AI systems. AI introduces risks involving prompt injection, data leakage, model manipulation, insecure agents and unauthorised AI activity.

Negative and harmful applications

AI-enabled cyberattacks Criminals can use AI to make phishing, social engineering and fraud more convincing and scalable.
Defensive AI can itself be attacked Security AI can be manipulated through poisoned data, adversarial inputs or malicious instructions.
False confidence Organisations may believe that purchasing an AI security platform automatically makes them secure, resulting in insufficient human oversight.
Privacy concerns Security systems may process large amounts of organisational and personal information, creating another important data-governance responsibility.

The global cybersecurity environment demonstrates why this trend is becoming increasingly important. INTERPOL's 2026 African Cyberthreat Assessment reports that AI is being used across the cybercrime lifecycle, including reconnaissance, phishing, extortion and evasion. The report indicates that 55% of surveyed cybercrime cases in Africa in 2025 involved AI occasionally or frequently.

This illustrates an important paradox: AI is becoming both a cybersecurity defence capability and a tool available to attackers. Organisations therefore cannot simply adopt AI without simultaneously strengthening governance, identity management, monitoring and incident-response capabilities.

Global perspective

Globally, attackers can use AI to make malicious activity more scalable and convincing. AI can assist criminals in creating realistic social engineering content, automating repetitive operations and adapting communications to specific targets.

The result is an expanding attack surface in which organisations must defend not only their traditional infrastructure but also their AI models, agents, data pipelines, prompts and integrations.

South African perspective

The South African context is particularly significant because the country is one of Africa's major technology and financial centres and is increasingly adopting AI across organisations.

At the same time, South Africa faces significant cybercrime exposure. INTERPOL's African Cyberthreat Assessment identifies AI as an increasingly important factor in the region's cybercrime environment.

South African organisations therefore need to treat AI security as part of their wider cybersecurity and governance strategy rather than as a standalone technology purchase.

South African Governance

Why governance matters in South Africa

The negative use of these technologies is not only a technical issue. It is also a governance, legal and societal issue. South Africa's Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA) places requirements on the processing and transfer of personal information. Section 72 regulates transfers of personal information to recipients in foreign countries and requires appropriate protection or another recognised legal basis for the transfer.

This becomes increasingly relevant as AI systems depend on cloud platforms, external models, APIs, development tools and third-party services. An organisation using an AI system must therefore understand not only what the model can do, but also what information is being processed, where that information goes and who can access it.

South Africa is also developing its national AI policy position. The 2026 Draft National AI Policy recognises that AI is developing rapidly and proposes a staged, sector-specific approach. The policy process emphasises ethical standards, socio-economic safeguards and collaboration between government, industry and other stakeholders.

Critical insight

South Africa should not approach emerging IT trends simply as technologies that need to be adopted. The country needs to ask whether the institutions adopting them have the skills, cybersecurity controls, data governance and human oversight required to use them responsibly.

This is particularly important because adoption is increasing faster than the development of some supporting capabilities. The opportunity is therefore not to reject AI, but to develop an environment in which innovation and responsible governance grow together.

Conclusion

The real risk is uncontrolled capability

The three trends examined in this analysis demonstrate that technological progress is not automatically positive or negative. Multiagent systems can automate complex processes, but autonomous actions can amplify errors, privilege abuse and data leakage. AI-native development platforms can democratise software creation, but they can also increase insecure code, reduce human oversight and place pressure on traditional developer learning pathways. AI security platforms can strengthen cyber defence, but they operate within an environment where attackers are simultaneously using AI to increase the scale and sophistication of attacks.

From a global perspective, these trends demonstrate the emergence of an increasingly AI-mediated digital environment. The distinction between software, automation and autonomous decision-making is becoming less clear. Organisations therefore need to move beyond traditional technology adoption models and consider security, accountability and human oversight from the beginning.

From a South African perspective, the issue is even more important because the country is simultaneously trying to increase AI adoption, strengthen its digital economy, develop technology skills and address cybersecurity risks. South Africa should take advantage of these technologies, but adoption must be accompanied by investment in digital skills, cybersecurity, data protection, AI governance and education.

Ultimately, the most important question is not whether these technologies should be adopted. It is whether they are being adopted responsibly. The same capabilities that make AI powerful for legitimate organisations can make it powerful for malicious actors. The future of IT therefore depends not only on developing more capable systems, but also on developing the governance, security and human expertise necessary to control those capabilities.
